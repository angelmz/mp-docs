Room 1 · Foundation

What Safety Is & the Culture of Safety

From slides 2–3, 15, 20 (NPG #2)

Your picture

Patient safety is the prevention of errors and adverse effects to patients associated with health care. It is freedom from psychological and physical injury, and it is a basic human need.

A culture of safety has six parts

Regulatory bodies

The Joint Commission, CMS, and state agencies set and enforce standards.

National Performance Goals

The yearly list of safety priorities (Room 6).

High-risk nature, with determination

Admit that health care is high-risk, and commit to consistently safe care anyway.

Blame-free environment

Staff can report errors and near misses without fear of punishment, so the system gets fixed.

Collaboration across levels

Everyone, from aide to administrator, works together on safety problems.

Committed resources

The organization pays for staffing, equipment, and training to stay safe.

The same idea shows up as

  • NPG #2, Culture of Safety: a safety program, a code of conduct, and a workplace violence program (Room 11)
  • Workplace culture as an individual risk: whether the workplace has a safety culture, resources, and accountability
Room 2 · Where the Patient Lives

Environmental Safety & Basic Needs

From slides 4–7, 44 (home basic needs)

Your picture

A patient's environment is all the physical and psychosocial factors that affect their life and survival. It goes wherever they go: hospital, long-term care facility, clinic, school, home.

Basic needs the environment must meet

Oxygen

  • Carbon monoxide: install CO detectors; never run heaters or cars in closed spaces
  • Home O2: no smoking or open flames. Oxygen feeds fire (Room 8).

Nutrition & food safety

  • Wash hands and surfaces; refrigerate leftovers promptly
  • Cook meats fully; check expiration dates

Temperature

  • Too hot: heat exhaustion, heat stroke
  • Too cold: frostbite, hypothermia
  • Infants and older adults are most at risk

Freedom from pathogens

  • Hand hygiene, immunizations, and preventing HAIs
  • Details in Room 10

Room 3 · Who Is at Risk: Age

Risks & Interventions Across the Lifespan

Risks from slides 10–14 paired with interventions from slides 38–42

Your picture

Each age shows its risks next to its interventions. The interventions answer the risks.

Infant, toddler, preschooler

Risks

  • SIDS
  • Poisoning, choking, aspiration
  • Falls, burns, drowning
  • Injuries are the leading cause of death after age 1

Interventions

  • Safe sleep: on the back, firm mattress, nothing soft in the crib
  • Feeding: avoid choking hazards (small, round, hard foods and objects)
  • Baby-proofing: lock up poisons and medicines, gate stairs, cover outlets
  • Swimming: never leave a child alone in or near water
  • Walking and running: supervise; stranger danger
  • Immunizations; car seats

School-age child

Risks

  • More confidence, less supervision
  • New environments and people
  • Expanded motor activities
  • School violence

Interventions

  • Bicycling and skateboarding: helmets and pads
  • Sports safety: proper equipment
  • Electrical equipment: teach safe use
  • Firearms safety: locked and unloaded, ammunition stored separately

Adolescent

Risks

  • More independence; forming an identity
  • Peer pressure; hormones
  • Substance abuse
  • Suicide (see NPG #8, Room 6)
  • Motor vehicle accidents

Interventions

  • Driver education
  • Teach about smoking, alcohol, and drugs
  • School and community activities; mentors
  • Internet safety

Adult

Risks

  • Lifestyle habits: stress, substance use, smoking

Interventions: address lifestyle factors

  • Childrearing stress; high stress overall
  • Inadequate nutrition
  • Firearms
  • Alcohol and drugs
  • Mental health

Older adult

Risks

  • Falls (Room 7)
  • Cognitive decline; dementia
  • Wandering

Interventions

  • Keep them as independent as possible
  • Fall prevention; exercise; safety equipment (grab bars, shower chair)
  • Vision and hearing exams
  • Medication organizers
  • Toileting schedules

Individual Risk Factors

From slides 15–17

Your picture

Lifestyle

  • Substance abuse
  • Dangerous jobs; reckless behavior; risk takers
  • Stress and anxiety (narrows attention)
  • Medications (drowsiness, dizziness)

Workplace culture

  • Is there a safety culture (Room 1)?
  • Are there resources and accountability?

Impaired mobility

  • Falls
  • Burns (can't move away from heat)
  • Can't get up to ask for help

Sensory, cognitive, or communication impairment

  • Falls
  • Motor vehicles (can't see or hear traffic)
  • Building structures (stairs, curbs)

Economic resources

  • Behavioral health issues; homelessness
  • Limited access to health care
  • Can't afford safe housing, equipment, or care

Lack of safety awareness

  • Lack of knowledge, or lack of care, about hazards
  • The fix is teaching
Room 5 · Finding the Risk

Nursing Assessment for Safety

From slides 33–35, 37 (fall assessment from slide 36 is in Room 7)

Your picture

Start patient-centered: ask them. What does the patient see as their risks? Have they fallen? What worries them at home?

1. The patient

Nursing history

Past falls, injuries, accidents; home situation.

Physical examination

Gait, balance, strength, vision, hearing, cognition.

Developmental status

Match the risks to the age (Room 3).

Medication review

Sedatives, opioids, BP drugs, diuretics, and many medicines at once raise risk (Room 9).

Cultural assessment

Beliefs and practices that shape safety choices.

Health literacy

Can they read and use the safety teaching you give?

2. The environment

Health care setting

  • Risk for medical errors (Room 9)
  • Disaster readiness (Room 11)

Home setting

  • Hazard assessment, room by room: lighting, rugs, stairs, bathroom, smoke and CO detectors, how medicines and chemicals are stored

3. Psychosocial & economic resources

  • Caregiver abilities: can the caregiver physically and mentally keep the patient safe?
  • Financial stability: can they afford safety equipment, medicines, and repairs? (Room 4)

Room 7 · The Biggest Hazard

Falls: Risk, Assessment & Prevention

Gathered from slides 7, 10, 14, 16, 25, 31, 36, 42, 44, 45 (side rails are in Room 12)

Your picture

About 11,000 fatal falls happen in health care facilities each year, plus many more injuries. Hospitals get no reimbursement from CMS for care caused by an in-hospital fall. Fall prevention is NPG #11.

Who falls

  • Older adults most of all (Room 3)
  • Infants and toddlers (falls from beds, changing tables, stairs)
  • Anyone with impaired mobility or a sensory or cognitive impairment (Room 4)

Intrinsic factors (the patient)

  • History of falls
  • Weakness, impaired gait or balance
  • Confusion, dementia
  • Medications; orthostatic hypotension
  • Urgency or incontinence
  • Poor vision

Extrinsic factors (the environment)

  • Wet or slippery floors
  • Clutter, cords, throw rugs
  • Poor lighting
  • Bed too high or unlocked
  • Poor footwear
  • Call light out of reach

When to do a fall risk assessment

1
On admission
2
With any change in condition
3
After a fall
4
On transfer to another unit or facility

Use a standardized assessment model, such as the Morse Fall Scale or Hendrich II.

Prevention in the hospital

Universal fall precautions (every patient)

  • Bed low and wheels locked
  • Call light and personal items within reach
  • Nonskid footwear
  • Room lit, clear, and uncluttered
  • Orient the patient to the room

For high-risk patients

  • Yellow armband marks fall risk
  • Hourly rounding: pain, position, potty, possessions
  • Toileting schedule
  • Bed or chair alarm; room near the nurses' station

Transportation

  • Lock wheelchair and stretcher wheels before transfers
  • Stretcher rails up and safety belts on
  • Enough staff to move the patient

Not a restraint replacement

  • Restraints and four side rails do not prevent falls and can make injuries worse (Room 12)

Prevention at home

  • Remove throw rugs and clutter; good lighting, including night lights
  • Grab bars, shower chair, raised toilet seat, handrails on stairs
  • Exercise for strength and balance; vision and hearing exams
  • Medication organizers and review of drugs that cause dizziness
Room 8 · Things That Burn, Shock & Poison

Fire, Electrical, Equipment & Chemical Hazards

Gathered from slides 7, 10, 29–30, 39, 44–45

Your picture

Fire

If there's a fire: RACE

  • Rescue patients in immediate danger
  • Alarm: activate it and call
  • Contain: close doors and windows
  • Extinguish or evacuate

Using an extinguisher: PASS

  • Pull the pin
  • Aim at the base of the fire
  • Squeeze the handle
  • Sweep side to side
  • Oxygen feeds fire: no smoking, candles, or open flames near O2, at home or in the hospital
  • Home: working smoke detectors, an escape plan, safe use of space heaters and stoves
  • Hospital: know where the alarms, extinguishers, and exits are; evacuate horizontally first

Electrical & equipment-related accidents

Caused by electrical hazards or malfunction, disrepair, or misuse of equipment.

1
Get adequate instructionNever use equipment you haven't been trained on.
2
Inspect before useFrayed cords, missing ground prongs, overdue inspection stickers. Use 3-prong grounded plugs.
3
Report and removeTag faulty equipment and take it out of service.
4
Preventive maintenance (PM)Biomedical engineering inspects equipment on a schedule.

For children (Room 3): teach safe use of electrical equipment and cover outlets.

Poison & chemical exposure

At home (mostly children)

  • Lock up medicines, cleaners, and chemicals
  • Keep products in their original containers
  • Post Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222

In health care (workers and patients)

  • Chemotherapy, cleaning agents, anesthetic gases
  • Safety Data Sheets (SDS) list each chemical's hazards, safe handling, PPE, and first aid
Room 9 · Errors Caused by Care

Procedure-Related Accidents & Medication Safety

Gathered from slides 15, 20 (NPG #1), 26 (NPG #14), 28, 34, 42, 44

Your picture

Procedure-related accidents are caused by health care providers: medication errors, improper application of devices, and improper performance of procedures. Most are prevented by following policy and procedure every time.

Right patient, right care (NPG #1)

  • Two identifiers before any medication, treatment, or specimen (name and DOB; never room number)
  • Label specimens in front of the patient
  • Hand-off communication at shift change and transfer, using a structured format like SBAR
  • Universal Protocol for surgery: pre-procedure verification, site marking, time-out

Managing medications (NPG #14)

  • Automatic dispensing cabinets
  • Standardized drug supply; clear labeling
  • Extra caution with anticoagulants
  • Antibiotic stewardship: use antibiotics only when needed, to prevent resistance

Medications across settings

Assessment

  • Medication review on every patient (Room 5)
  • Medications are an individual risk factor (drowsiness, dizziness)

Home

  • Medication organizers, especially for older adults
  • Keep medicines in original, labeled bottles and locked away from children

Transmission of Pathogens

From slides 8, 22 (NPG #5), 44

Your picture

Handwashing

The single most effective way to prevent the spread of infection.

Health care-acquired infection (HAI)

An infection the patient did not have on admission and got during care.

Immunizations

Protect patients and staff; part of child safety (Room 3).

Medical asepsis at home

Clean technique: hand hygiene, clean surfaces, safe food handling.

NPG #5: every facility runs an infection prevention and control program that complies with CDC and WHO guidance.

Room 11 · Sudden Threats

Workplace Violence, Disasters & Seizures

Gathered from slides 7, 20–21 (NPG #2, #3), 32, 35, 45

Your picture

Workplace violence

  • Can come from patients, visitors, intruders, or co-workers
  • Ranges from psychological harm (threats, bullying) all the way to death
  • NPG #2 requires a workplace violence program
  • Nurse's role: recognize escalation early, de-escalate, keep an exit, call security, report every incident

Disasters & emergency readiness

  • Natural (hurricane, flood, tornado) or man-made (mass shooting, bioterrorism, chemical spill)
  • NPG #3: every facility has an emergency operations plan and tests it with periodic drills
  • NPG #11: emergency back-up power during outages
  • Know your role in the plan before a disaster happens

Seizures

Seizure precautions (before)

  • Bed low; padded side rails
  • Suction and oxygen ready at the bedside

During a seizure

  • Stay with the patient; call for help
  • Protect the head; turn on the side to keep the airway open
  • Nothing in the mouth; do not restrain
  • Time it, then document what you saw
Room 12 · The Last Resort

Restraints & Side Rails

From slides 45–55 · Box 27.12, p. 434

Your picture

A restraint is any manual method, physical or mechanical device, material, or equipment that immobilizes or reduces a patient's ability to move arms, legs, body, or head freely.

The goal is a restraint-free environment.

Is a restraint

  • Wrist, ankle, vest, or mitt restraints
  • All 4 side rails up on a patient who can't lower them
  • Chemical restraint: a drug used to control behavior or movement that is not standard treatment for the patient's condition

Is not a restraint

  • Orthopedically prescribed devices
  • Protective helmets
  • Physically holding a patient to do an exam
  • Protecting a patient from falling out of bed
  • Letting a patient join activities without risk of harm

Rules for use

  • Temporary
  • Last resort, after alternatives have failed
  • Least restrictive option that works

Try these alternatives first (Box 27.12)

Reassure & watch

  • Orient and explain
  • Sitters
  • Room close to the nurses' station
  • De-escalation

Meet needs

  • Pain control
  • Physiological needs (toileting, hunger, thirst)
  • Relaxation, rest; exercise

Distract

  • Diversion activities
  • Visual and auditory stimuli (music, TV)

Remove the trigger

  • Camouflage IV lines; discontinue bothersome treatments
  • Remove cues (for example, a coat that prompts leaving)
  • Protective devices
Complications are serious, even death Pneumonia, pressure injuries, strangulation, loss of strength and function, incontinence, increased agitation. Staff training on restraints is mandatory.

Legal implications

  • Know your agency policy and procedures
  • Must be clinically justified and part of the prescribed medical treatment
  • A provider evaluates the patient face-to-face
  • The order must be current and state the type and location, duration, and circumstances
  • Never a PRN order

Time frames are CMS rules as commonly taught. Confirm the exact numbers your instructor uses.

Required nursing assessment components (check often, per policy)

  • Circulation below the restraint: pulse, color, temperature, sensation
  • Skin integrity under the restraint
  • Range of motion: release and move the limb
  • Toileting, food, and fluids
  • Behavior and whether the restraint is still needed
  • Two fingers fit under the restraint
  • Tie with a quick-release knot to the bed frame, never the side rail

Removal

Remove as soon as the patient meets the criteria. Once removed, reapplying needs a new order.

Side rails

Benefits

  • Help the patient turn and reposition
  • Hold bed controls
  • Protect during transport or after sedation

Risks

  • All 4 up = a restraint
  • Entrapment between rail and mattress
  • Climbing over a rail makes a fall worse (Room 7)

Ten Things to Carry Out of This Chapter

  1. Safety is a basic human need: freedom from psychological and physical injury.
  2. A culture of safety is blame-free: report errors so the system gets fixed.
  3. Injuries are the leading cause of death in children over 1; falls are the big risk for older adults.
  4. Match every age's risks to its interventions: safe sleep for SIDS, driver education for MVAs, fall prevention for older adults.
  5. Assess the patient (ask them first), the environment, and their caregiver and finances.
  6. Know the 14 National Performance Goals. #1 is two identifiers, hand-off, and Universal Protocol.
  7. Assess fall risk on admission, change in condition, after a fall, and on transfer. Yellow armband, hourly rounding, bed low and locked, call light in reach.
  8. Fire: RACE, then PASS. Oxygen feeds fire.
  9. Report and remove faulty equipment. Check the SDS for chemical hazards.
  10. Restraints are the last resort: least restrictive, temporary, never PRN, ordered with type, location, duration, and circumstances.
