Room 1 · Foundation

Nutrition, Nutrients & Energy Balance

From slides 3–4

Your picture

Nutrition is the body's intake and use of adequate amounts of necessary nutrients for tissue growth and energy production.

Nutrients are the substances in food that supply energy; build and maintain bones, muscles, and skin; and support normal growth and function of every body system.

Energy balance: intake vs. what the body needs

Too little for the body's needs

  • Malnutrition: an imbalance between nutrient intake and the body's needs
  • Details in Room 8

Too much for the body's use

  • Obesity: energy intake consistently exceeds energy use
  • Details in Room 8

Food security means access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food. Without it, balance is hard to reach no matter what the patient knows.

Room 2 · What's in the Food: Large Amounts

Macronutrients: Carbs, Protein, Fat, Water

From slides 6–12

Your picture

Macronutrients are needed in large amounts. Carbohydrates, proteins, and fats supply energy, measured in kilocalories (1 kcal = 1,000 calories; "calories" on a label means kcal). Water supplies none but is 60–70% of body weight.

Nutrientkcal per gramMain job
Carbohydrate4Main source of energy
Protein4Build, maintain, repair tissue
Fat9Most calorie-dense; energy storage
Water0Temperature, transport, fluid balance

Carbohydrates

Sugars, starches, and fiber. They turn into glucose, the fuel for the brain, for skeletal muscle during exercise, and for blood cell production.

Simple carbs

  • Broken down and absorbed quickly
  • Fast energy that burns off fast
  • Glucose, fructose, sucrose, lactose, maltose (sugars)

Complex carbs

  • Starches, glycogen, and fiber
  • Take longer to break down before use
  • Energy lasts longer

Fiber (a complex carb)

  • Soluble or insoluble, depending on how it behaves when mixed with water
  • Older children, adolescents, and adults: 20–35 g per day
  • Sources: whole grains, wheat bran, cereals, fresh fruits, vegetables, legumes

Protein

  • Develops, maintains, and repairs tissues, organs, and cells
  • Made of amino acids, the "building blocks." There are 9 essential amino acids.
  • Amino acids are not stored. They must be eaten every day.
  • If carbs or fat are deficient, the body burns protein for energy instead

Complete protein

  • Contains all essential amino acids
  • Animal foods: eggs, cheese, fish, poultry, red meat
  • Plus soybeans, the plant exception

Incomplete protein

  • Missing one or more essential amino acids
  • Vegetables, legumes, seeds, nuts, grains

The lab value for protein status (albumin) is in Room 7.

Fats (lipids)

Fats are essential for

  • Energy production and storage in adipose tissue
  • Support and insulation of major organs and nerve fibers
  • Lubrication of body tissues
  • Absorbing and transporting vitamins A, D, E, and K (Room 3)
  • Building cell membranes
  • Lipid: any fat in the body, including fatty acids, cholesterol, and phospholipids
  • Triglycerides: the most abundant lipids in food
  • Cholesterol: a waxy, fat-like substance found in every cell

Unsaturated ("good")

  • Olive oil, avocado, almonds, walnuts

Saturated & trans ("bad")

  • Butter, cake, ice cream, packaged cookies
  • Raise total cholesterol

Water

  • 60–70% of total body weight
  • Controls body temperature; maintains acid-base balance; regulates fluid and electrolytes; transports nutrients and waste
  • Sources: drinks, plus foods high in water (fresh fruits and vegetables)
  • Intake must equal output to stay in balance
Room 3 · What's in the Food: Small Amounts

Micronutrients: Vitamins & Minerals

From slides 6, 13–16

Your picture

Micronutrients are needed in very small amounts, but a deficiency can be life-threatening. Vitamins are organic compounds that regulate body processes, reproduction, and growth. Minerals support energy, muscle building, nerve conduction, clotting, and immunity.

Fat-soluble: A, D, E, K

  • Need fat to be absorbed (Room 2)
  • Stored in the body
  • Too much → hypervitaminosis, which can be toxic

Water-soluble: B complex, C

  • Excreted in urine
  • Not stored, so must be eaten daily

Vitamin A

  • Night vision, resistance to infection
  • Grows epithelial tissue, bones, teeth
  • Deficiency: night blindness, low immunity, impaired growth

Vitamin D

  • The "sunshine vitamin"
  • Vital for bone and tissue development

Vitamin E

  • Antioxidant: protects cells from free radicals (cell damage from things like x-ray exposure)
  • Supports a healthy immune system

Vitamin K

  • Makes the proteins that clot blood
  • Deficiency: bruising and bleeding

Vitamin C

  • Antioxidant
  • Builds collagen; strong immune system

Vitamin B complex

  • Helps produce energy; acts as coenzymes
  • Helps form red blood cells

Minerals & electrolytes

Potassium, sodium, chloride

  • Fluid balance
  • Nerve conduction and muscle contraction

Calcium, phosphorus, magnesium

  • Build and maintain bone
  • Nerve conduction and muscle contraction
Room 4 · What the Body Does With It

Digestion, Metabolism & BMR

From slides 4, 17 (and speaker notes)

Your picture

The path of food, in order

1
IngestionConsuming food.
2
DigestionBreaking food down into smaller nutrient particles.
3
AbsorptionSmall particles move through the digestive tract wall into the blood.
4
MetabolismNutrients are chemically changed into energy or stored for later. This keeps the body in homeostasis.
5
Peristalsis → eliminationWavelike muscle movement pushes waste through the intestines.

Textbook figure 45.1, p. 1188.

Metabolism keeps two forces in balance

Anabolism: builds up

  • Builds more complex substances
  • Builds tissue, such as adding lean muscle with diet and exercise

Catabolism: breaks down

  • Breaks substances into simpler ones the body can absorb and use
  • Too much leads to starvation

Where the energy goes

  • Mechanical energy for muscle contraction
  • Electrical energy for the nervous system
  • Thermal energy for body heat

Where extra is stored

  • Fat → adipose tissue
  • Protein → muscle
  • Glucose → liver (as glycogen)
Basal metabolic rate (BMR) The minimum energy needed to keep the body running at rest while awake: breathing, circulation, heart rate, temperature regulation.

Room 6 · Who Is Eating

Factors Influencing Nutrition: Lifespan, Religion & Culture

From slides 21–22 (and speaker notes)

Your picture

Across the lifespan, youngest to oldest

1
PregnancyPoor nutrition → low birth weight and lower survival. Protein, calcium, iron, and folic acid are crucial to prevent birth defects and anemia.
2
Infants: breast milk or formulaBreastfeeding is strongly supported for the first 6 months: fewer food allergies, easier digestion, convenient, less expensive. Formula has about the same nutrients but costs more. No whole cow's milk in the first year.
3
Infants: starting solids at 4–6 monthsIron-rich cereal first. Introduce one new food a week, careful with high-allergy foods. Finger foods build fine-motor skills.
4
ToddlersNeed more protein. Vegetables are hard to get in; role modeling helps.
5
AdolescentsMore protein; calcium for bone growth; iron (girls to replace menstrual loss, boys for muscle). Body-image concerns, fast food, and fad diets.
6
Older adultsThe challenge is choosing nutrient-dense foods. Metabolism slows; appetite, taste, smell, and thirst decline; GI issues; income and transportation limit grocery shopping.
Older adult food ideas Cream soups, meat-and-vegetable soups, cheese, eggs, peanut butter. Calcium (dairy, leafy greens, soy, nuts, fish) to prevent osteoporosis. A multivitamin with vitamin D. Avoid grapefruit juice, which interferes with medication absorption.

Religion & culture

Patient-centered care means respecting dietary needs from cultural or religious traditions different from your own. Ask; don't assume.

Common religious restrictions

  • Judaism (kosher): no pork or shellfish; meat and dairy not eaten together
  • Islam (halal): no pork or alcohol; daytime fasting during Ramadan
  • Hinduism: no beef; many are vegetarian
  • Buddhism: many are vegetarian
  • Seventh-day Adventist: often vegetarian; no alcohol or caffeine
  • Latter-day Saints (Mormon): no alcohol, coffee, or tea
  • Catholicism: no meat on Fridays during Lent

Check these against your textbook: Table 45.3 and Box 45.5, p. 1195.

Infants

Adults

A protein in the blood that reflects protein intake. Low albumin suggests malnutrition or low protein intake, which slows wound healing and weakens immune function.

3. Ask: diet & health history

Ask every patient about typical intake, food preferences, and allergies. Also screen: age, cultural preferences, activity level, alcohol and illicit drugs, prescription and OTC medications (Box 45.6, p. 1199).

24-hour recall

  • Everything eaten in the previous day
  • Depends on the patient's memory of foods and amounts

Food diary

  • 3 to 5 days of entries
  • Must include a typical weekend

Allergy vs. intolerance

Food allergy

  • Immune system response: the body wrongly sees a food as harmful and makes antibodies
  • Life-threatening
  • Common: peanuts, shellfish, wheat; alpha-gal (red meat, after a Lone Star tick bite)

Food intolerance

  • Digestive system response: irritation, or inability to break the food down
  • Uncomfortable, not life-threatening
  • Lactose intolerance; gluten (celiac: wheat, rye, barley, oats)
Room 8 · When Balance Fails

Malnutrition, Obesity & Physical Signs

From slides 23, 25, 35–36

Your picture

Too little

  • Malnutrition: from digestion or absorption problems, illness, increased metabolic needs, or inadequate or imbalanced intake
  • Cachexia: physical wasting with weight and muscle loss, often in terminal illness when the person can't eat enough
  • Screen with the MNA; check albumin (Room 7)

Too much

  • Overweight: BMI 25–29.9
  • Obesity: BMI 30 or higher
  • Morbid obesity: BMI over 40, or more than 50% above ideal body weight
  • BMI shows the extent of obesity and its health risks
Nursing problemOverweightBased on BMI. Plan with the patient and a dietitian.

Psychological causes

  • Anorexia: loss of appetite (a symptom)
  • Anorexia nervosa: a serious disorder; life-threatening restriction driven by an altered mental state
  • Bulimia nervosa: bingeing (eating excessive food) followed by purging (vomiting)

Room 9 · Getting Food In Safely

Dysphagia, Aspiration & Feeding Assistance

From slides 31–34

Your picture

Dysphagia is difficulty swallowing. It can lead to malnutrition. Aspiration is inhaling fluid or foreign matter into the lungs and bronchi.

How it progresses

1
CauseOften residual effects of stroke or other injury (Box 45.7, p. 1200).
2
Signs of dysphagiaCoughing while eating; change in voice tone or quality; abnormal gag; drooling; incomplete lip closure; poor tongue control; excessive chewing; gagging before swallowing; failure to swallow; pocketing (food held in the cheek); regurgitation; refusing to eat.
3
Signs of aspiration: monitor closelyCoughing, wheezing, dyspnea, apnea, bradycardia, hypotension. Stroke patients may have silent aspiration with no cough.
4
ConsequencesAspiration pneumonia, dehydration, poor nutrition status, weight loss.

Screening and testing

  • Bedside dysphagia screen: quickly finds swallowing problems so you can consult a registered dietitian or speech pathologist
  • Swallow study: for anyone at risk of aspiration
  • May lead to a thickened liquids diet (Room 10)
Nursing problemImpaired Swallowing
Nursing problemRisk for Aspiration

Assistance with feeding

Who needs help: patients with limited hand or arm mobility, poor activity tolerance, poor cognitive or physical state, or poor vision.

  • Elevate the head of the bed to 90 degrees unless contraindicated (aspiration precautions skill, pp. 1219–1221)
  • Watch the patient's ability to swallow and for the dysphagia signs above
  • Use adaptive equipment: built-up utensils, divided plates, two-handled cups (Fig. 45.7)
  • Visually impaired: describe the plate like a clock ("meat at 12, green beans at 3")
  • Note any difficulty the patient or caregiver has with feeding
Document Food intake as a percentage eaten (0–100%). Liquids in milliliters.

Walk-through · Review

Ten Things to Carry Out of This Chapter

  1. Nutrition is a balance: too little intake for the body's needs is malnutrition; too much for its use is obesity.
  2. Carbs and protein are 4 kcal/g; fat is 9. Carbs are the main fuel.
  3. Amino acids and water-soluble vitamins (B, C) are not stored, so they are needed every day.
  4. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) need fat to absorb and can build up to toxic levels.
  5. Vitamin K clots, A sees at night, D builds bone, C builds collagen, B makes red blood cells.
  6. Read labels by condition: sodium for HTN, saturated fat and cholesterol for high cholesterol, carbs and sugar for diabetes.
  7. An allergy is an immune response and can kill; an intolerance is a digestive response and is uncomfortable.
  8. BMI: 18.5–24.9 normal, 25–29.9 overweight, 30+ obese, 40+ morbid obesity. MNA: 12–14 normal, 8–11 at risk, 0–7 malnourished.
  9. Dysphagia leads to aspiration. Watch for coughing, voice change, and pocketing; stroke patients can aspirate silently. HOB at 90 degrees.
  10. Advance diets one rung at a time: NPO → clear → full liquid → pureed → mechanical soft → regular.
