Room 1 · Foundation

Sterile Technique: What, Why, When

From slides 3–5

Your picture

Sterile technique eliminates all microorganisms from an object or area. It is a series of steps, done in the operating room or at the bedside.

Why we do it

  • Prevents contamination of an open wound
  • Isolates the procedure area from the unsterile environment
  • Maintains a sterile field for the procedure

When it is required

  • Procedures that intentionally perforate the skin
  • Any time skin integrity is broken
  • During invasive procedures

Clean technique

  • Reduces the number of microorganisms
  • Clean gloves
  • Example: oropharyngeal suctioning (Yankauer)

Sterile technique

  • Eliminates all microorganisms
  • Sterile field, sterile gloves
  • Examples: nasopharyngeal suctioning, wound care, wound irrigation

Every procedure in the rooms that follow is either clean or sterile. Check which one before you start.

Room 2 · The Workspace

The Sterile Field

From slides 6–10

Your picture

A sterile field is an area considered free of microorganisms, needed whenever the nurse performs a sterile procedure. Preparing one combines four skills: opening sterile packages, laying a sterile drape, adding sterile supplies, and pouring sterile solutions.

The 7 principles of sterile technique

1
Sterile touching sterile stays sterile.A sterile object remains sterile when touched by another sterile object.
2
Only sterile goes on the field.Only sterile objects may be placed on a sterile field.
3
Below the waist or out of sight = contaminated.Keep the field above waist level and in your line of vision. Never turn your back on it.
4
Air exposure contaminates over time.Prolonged exposure to air contaminates a sterile object or field.
5
Wet wicks contamination.A sterile surface touching a wet, contaminated surface is contaminated by capillary action.
6
Fluid flows with gravity.Keep tips of wet items pointed down so fluid doesn't run from your contaminated hand back to the sterile tip.
7
The edges are contaminated.The outer 1-inch border of a sterile field is considered contaminated.

Setting up, in order

1
Gather all equipment first.Reaching away mid-procedure risks contamination.
2
Inspect every package.Check for tears, moisture, and expiration date.
3
Open on a clean, flat surface, as close as possible to the time of use.Less air exposure (principle 4).
4
Open the first flap AWAY from you.So you never reach across the sterile inside.
5
Add supplies and pour solutions without touching or splashing the field.
When to stop and start over Discard and restart if the field or an item touches anything non-sterile, liquid splatters onto it, or a strike-through happens (liquid soaks through the field, so it is contaminated).

Patient education: keep the patient from contaminating the field

  • Avoid sudden movements of body parts covered by sterile drapes
  • Do not touch sterile supplies, drapes, or the nurse's gloves/gown
  • Avoid coughing, sneezing, or talking over the sterile area

Sterile Gloves: Open Gloving

From slides 11–12

Your picture

Remember "sterile to sterile." Your bare hand touches only the inside of the glove. A gloved hand touches only the outside of the other glove.

  • An additional barrier to bacterial transfer; helps prevent transmission of pathogens
  • Always apply before performing a sterile procedure
  • Choose the right size and the right material (ask about latex allergy)

Skill: open gloving, in order

1
Hand hygieneAt least 20 seconds.
2
Open the outer packageOn a clean, dry, flat surface at waist level or above.
3
Open the inner wrapperTouch only the outer edges; fold the flaps back so the gloves lie exposed.
4
Identify right and leftCuffs face you.
5
First glove (dominant hand)Nondominant thumb and fingers grasp the folded cuff (the inside surface). Pull the glove on without touching its outside.
6
Slide under the second cuffGloved fingers go under the cuff of the second glove, touching only its outside.
7
Pull on the second gloveKeep the gloved thumb pointed away so it doesn't touch bare skin.
8
Adjust and holdInterlock fingers to adjust. Keep hands above the waist and in sight (field principle 3).
Room 4 · The Airway

Suctioning: Oropharyngeal & Nasopharyngeal

From slides 13–23 (both documentation slides merged)

Your picture

When to suction

  • The client cannot clear their own airway
  • Decreased level of consciousness (LOC)
  • The client is weak or critically ill

Equipment

  • PPE
  • Normal saline or water
  • Suction catheters
  • Disposable cup or basin
  • Suction machine or wall suction
  • Connecting tubing

Hazards of suctioning

Hypoxia

Suction removes oxygen along with secretions. Limit each pass and reoxygenate.

Cardiac dysrhythmia

Follows hypoxia or vagal stimulation. Watch heart rate and SpO2.

Vagal stimulation

The catheter in the throat can drop the heart rate (bradycardia).

Cough reflex stimulation

Coughing and gagging are expected, but they add stress for a weak client.

The two types, side by side

OropharyngealNasopharyngeal
RouteMouthNose
DeviceYankauer (rigid tip)Flexible suction catheter
TechniqueCleanSterile
WhenSecretions in the mouth and throatYankauer is ineffective or inappropriate, or the lower airway needs secretions removed
Tips
  • Coughing, gagging, sneezing are normal
  • Be ready to reapply supplemental O2
  • Store the Yankauer in a non-airtight container
  • Use water-soluble lubricant
  • No suction while inserting (thumb off the port)
  • Intermittent suction for up to 10–15 seconds
  • Rest at least 1 minute between attempts
  • 2 passes max per catheter
Instructor pearls Nose → mouth is OK. Mouth → nose is never OK. The mouth is dirtier, so a catheter that has been in the mouth must not go into the nose.
Insert about 6.5 inches (not meters). The kit is marked in centimeters.

Documentation

Both types

  • Amount, consistency, color, and odor of secretions
  • Client's response to the procedure
  • Pre- and post-suction cardiopulmonary assessment
  • Instructions given to caregivers and their ability to perform correctly

Oropharyngeal adds

  • Number of times suctioned

Nasopharyngeal adds

  • Size of catheter
  • Route of suctioning
  • Pre- and post-suction vital signs
  • Breath sounds
  • SpO2 level

Wound Assessment, Drainage & Complications

From slides 24–28, plus the pressure dressing from slide 33

Your picture

Wound bed assessment

  • Location
  • Type of wound
  • Appearance before and after care
  • Size
  • Approximation (are the edges together?)
  • Periwound area (the skin around the wound)
  • Amount, color, and odor of drainage, using the four types below

The four drainage types (slide 26 photos)

Serous

Clear to pale yellow, thin and watery. Plasma.

Serosanguineous

Pale pink to light red, watery. A mix of plasma and blood.

Sanguineous

Bright red. Fresh bleeding.

Purulent

Thick; yellow, green, tan, or brown; may smell. Pus, which signals infection.

Complications

Listed in order of severity, from bleeding to organs outside the body.

Hemorrhage

  • Bleeding from the wound; dressing saturates quickly
  • Watch for signs of shock: rising HR, falling BP
  • Response: pressure dressing (below)

Infection

  • Redness, warmth, swelling, pain
  • Purulent drainage, odor, fever
  • Prevention is the reason for every rule in Rooms 1–3

Dehiscence

  • Wound edges separate (partial or total)
  • Often after coughing or straining
  • Increase in serosanguineous drainage can be the warning

Evisceration

  • Wound opens and organs protrude
  • Emergency: stay with the patient, call for help
  • Cover with sterile gauze moistened with sterile saline
  • Notify the surgeon; keep NPO

Hemorrhage response: pressure dressing, in order

1
Apply direct pressureTemporary control of excessive, sudden, unanticipated bleeding.
2
Cover with multiple thicknesses of gauze
3
Tape over the gauze
4
Wrap an elastic rolled bandage over the gauzeThen check circulation below the wrap (see Room 8).
5
Report to the provider
6
Document

Wound Care, Dressings & Irrigation

From slides 29–32, 34–36

Your picture

Before you start

  • Check for an order before removing a surgical dressing
  • Give pain medication at least 30 minutes before (wound care and irrigation)
  • Use aseptic technique; keep a disposal bag and extra gloves within reach
  • Remove tape slowly, pulling toward the wound

Cleaning direction: always clean to dirty

  • Incision: start on the incision line and work outward; wipe top to bottom, one stroke per gauze
  • Drain site: start at the drain and clean in circles moving outward

Dry vs. moist dressings: removal is the opposite

Dry dressing

  • For abrasions and non-draining postoperative incisions
  • Not used for debridement
  • If it sticks to the wound, moisten it before removing

Moist dressing ("wet-to-dry" or "moist-to-dry")

  • Gauze moistened with the ordered solution as a moist contact layer
  • Mechanically debrides the wound and promotes healing
  • Do not moisten the inner layer to remove it. Pulling it off dry is what debrides.

Skill: moist-to-dry dressing change, in order

1
PrepareCheck the order, premedicate for pain, perform hand hygiene, provide privacy, position the patient.
2
Remove the old dressing with clean glovesNote drainage on it, then discard dressing and gloves.
3
Set up the sterile fieldPour the solution over the gauze in the sterile tray (Room 2).
4
Sterile gloves onSee Room 3.
5
Assess and clean the woundSee Room 5, then clean to dirty.
6
Pack moist gauze into the woundWring it out until damp, not dripping. Fill the wound loosely without touching the surrounding skin.
7
Cover with dry gauze and secure
8
DocumentWound assessment, drainage, what was used, patient's tolerance.

Other dressings

Transparent dressing

  • Thin polyurethane sheet with hypoallergenic acrylic adhesive on one side
  • Semi-occlusive: clear, adherent, non-absorptive
  • Conforms to body contours; you can see the wound through it
  • Moisture inactivates the adhesive

Wound VAC

  • Foam or gauze plus negative (subatmospheric) pressure through suction
  • Pulls out wound fluid, reduces edema, stimulates repair
  • Runs intermittently or continuously
  • Must stay sealed or the negative pressure is lost

Wound irrigation (sterile)

  • Delivers cleaning solution directly into the wound with a syringe, syringe and catheter, pulsed lavage device, or handheld shower
  • Position for gravity: solution flows over the wound into a collection basin (field principle 6)
  • Keep the syringe tip 1 inch above the wound
  • Pain medication at least 30 minutes before
Room 7 · Getting Fluid Out

Drains: Open & Closed

From slides 29, 37–38

Your picture

Open drain

Penrose

  • Flat, soft tube; drains onto the dressing
  • A safety pin keeps it from slipping into the wound
  • Dressing gets wet, so change or reinforce it more often

Closed drains (suction)

Jackson-Pratt (JP)

  • Tubing to a small bulb
  • Squeeze the bulb and cap it to create suction

Hemovac

  • Larger, round spring-loaded canister
  • Compress it and close the port to create suction

Drain care

  • Clean the drain site from the drain outward in circles (Room 6)
  • Empty a closed drain when it is about half full; measure and record output and drainage type (Room 5)
  • Re-compress the bulb or canister after emptying so suction continues
Room 8 · The Outer Layer

Binders & Elastic Bandages

From slides 39–41

Your picture

Shared rules. Binders and bandages hold dressings securely. Over a dressing they add protection and therapeutic benefit. They must not injure underlying or nearby parts, cause discomfort, or reduce breathing. If a physician applied it, get an order before loosening or removing it.

Binders

  • Support underlying muscles and large incisions
  • Most common: abdominal binder
  • Wraps the trunk, so assess ventilation frequently

Elastic bandages

  • Hold dressings, apply compression, or wrap hard-to-cover areas
  • Turns and width depend on the size and shape of the body part
  • Wrap distal to proximal
  • Not too tight
  • Keep toes or fingertips uncovered and visible to check circulation
The check after any wrap Binder: is breathing restricted? Bandage: are the exposed fingers or toes warm, pink, with good capillary refill and sensation?
